KOL Profiling Engine for an IBD Advisory Board

Brief: Profiled 51 IBD key opinion leaders across US and ex-US markets for a pharma advisory board and recommended its strategic discussion themes. Delivered in 15 days against a two-month manual baseline; now being built into Lucid's Fusion platform.

Situation
  • Pharma client preparing an advisory board for an asset in inflammatory bowel disease.
  • IBD is one of the most crowded fields in immunology: 10+ advanced therapies, no biomarker to predict response.
  • Live KOL debates are about sequencing, how early and aggressively to treat, and safety trade-offs between classes.
  • Client needed to know where each expert stood on those debates, not just who they were.
Complication
  • Data scattered: H1 for clinical and publication records; congress activity (ECCO, DDW, UEG Week), social media and recent positions spread across LinkedIn, X and journal sites.
  • Manual profiling of 51 KOLs was a two-month job, with inconsistent output across analysts.
  • Client wanted an agenda, not a directory: the 51 profiles had to be synthesised, not just described.
Solution
  1. Designed a standard profile template: clinical focus, institutional role, publication themes, congress activity, digital voice, stated positions on the asset and indication.
  2. Pulled core profiles and publications from H1; used Claude Cowork to extract and structure social and congress activity against the template, with human QC on every profile.
  3. Synthesised across all 51 to surface recurring themes, disagreements and emerging topics; translated them into ad-board discussion themes, each mapped to the KOLs most likely to drive it.
Impact
  • 15 days of effort vs. a two-month baseline (~75% faster).
  • 51 consistent, evidence-backed profiles and an ad-board agenda grounded in what the experts are actually saying.
  • Template and workflow being productised into Lucid's Fusion platform for asset- and indication-tailored KOL profiles.

Disease contextTap to open: what IBD is and how it is treated
What IBD is
  • Two chronic, relapsing gut conditions: Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis (UC).
  • Driven by an exaggerated immune response to the gut microbiome in genetically susceptible people.
  • Onset typically in the 20s–30s; no cure; ~7 million patients worldwide; goal is to induce and hold remission for decades.
Crohn's vs. UC
  • UC: continuous inflammation confined to the colon lining; colectomy is curative but life-altering.
  • Crohn's: patchy, anywhere mouth to anus, through the full bowel wall; strictures, fistulas and abscesses; surgery common but not curative.
  • UC is usually the proof-of-concept indication for a new mechanism; Crohn's follows 1–2 years later.
Treatment landscape
  • Base: 5-ASAs, steroids, immunomodulators.
  • Advanced: anti-TNFs (now biosimilar), anti-integrin vedolizumab, IL-23 inhibitors (current growth engine), oral JAK and S1P drugs.
  • Pipeline: anti-TL1A antibodies and obefazimod in phase 3.
  • Most monotherapies plateau at 30–40% remission, which drives the sequencing and combination debates.
In-licensing Due Diligence · Immunology · HS & axSpA · Ongoing strategic support

Asset Due Diligence

Brief: Evaluated two early-stage bispecific antibodies, TNF × IL-17A/F and TL1A × IL-17A, for hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) and axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) on behalf of a biotech client building its pipeline through in-licensing. Client signed non-binding term sheets on both assets and has since closed its Series B.

Situation
  • Biotech client that builds its pipeline by in-licensing rather than in-house discovery; we provide ongoing strategic support on asset evaluation.
  • Two early-stage bispecifics identified, each pairing IL-17A with a second validated axis, aimed at HS and axSpA.
  • Both indications have hit a response ceiling with single-target biologics; dual-cytokine bispecifics are the industry's bet on moving it.
  • Before a term sheet, client needed: scientific credibility, differentiation vs. incumbents and competitors, and the indications where each asset could win.
Complication
  • Early-stage assets, so the case had to be built from component evidence.
  • TNF × IL-17A/F: strong rationale (both pathways proven in HS and axSpA) but poor precedent; earlier TNF × IL-17 bispecifics stalled at phase 2, one for failing to beat adalimumab, one on safety.
  • TL1A × IL-17A: attractive biology (TL1A amplifies Th17; possible anti-fibrotic effect on HS tunnels) but TL1A is only validated in IBD; no human data in HS or axSpA.
  • Class safety signals: IL-17 blockade can trigger candidiasis and flare IBD, relevant given gut inflammation in axSpA and HS populations; dual blockade has historically added infection risk.
  • Dense competition: an IL-17A/F antibody already approved in both indications; IL-17, IL-1 and JAK programmes in late-stage HS trials; big-pharma TL1A programmes that could expand beyond IBD.
  • No like-for-like benchmark: trials used different endpoints (HiSCR, ASAS40), populations and durations.
Solution
  1. Mapped MoA and target biology for TNF, IL-17A/F and TL1A, including where pathways converge or could undermine each other.
  2. Built an indication-by-target evidence matrix: proven, failed, open, across HS, axSpA and adjacent indications.
  3. Assessed scientific differentiation vs. incumbents; mapped every competitor programme by target, modality and stage.
  4. Normalised efficacy and safety benchmarks across pivotal trials and compared study designs so results read like-for-like.
  5. Reviewed class and combination safety signals, using discontinued dual-cytokine programmes as precedents.
  6. Profiled every approved and discontinued asset per target, with reasons for discontinuation.
  7. Recommended where each asset's dual-target rationale is strongest and what it must demonstrate to be differentiated.
Impact
  • Client moved forward on both assets: non-binding term sheets signed to in-license the TNF × IL-17A/F and TL1A × IL-17A bispecifics.
  • Client has since closed its Series B with those assets in the pipeline story.
  • Evidence matrix, competitor map and benchmark structure now the standing framework for the client's subsequent asset reviews; engagement continues.
Disease & market contextTap to open: the immunology market, HS and axSpA
Why bispecifics, why now
  • Immunology is the largest therapeutic market after oncology.
  • Anti-TNFs that built the market are now biosimilar; growth comes from IL-17, IL-23 and oral JAKs.
  • Bispecifics that block two cytokines at once are the next wave, aimed at a response ceiling single-target drugs have not moved in a decade.
Hidradenitis suppurativa
  • Chronic, painful inflammatory skin disease: blocked, inflamed hair follicles in armpits, groin and buttocks form recurring abscesses and tunnels.
  • ~1% prevalence; typically diagnosed 7–10 years late.
  • Adalimumab was the only approved biologic until 2023; IL-17 antibodies since added; ~half of patients still miss a meaningful response (HiSCR).
  • Crowded pipeline: IL-17, IL-1 and JAK programmes.
Axial spondyloarthritis
  • Chronic inflammatory arthritis of the spine and sacroiliac joints; strongly HLA-B27-linked; starts in young adults; can progress to spinal fusion.
  • TNF blockers the backbone since the early 2000s; IL-17 antibodies and JAK inhibitors added.
  • IL-23 blockade works in psoriasis and Crohn's but failed in axSpA: pathways don't transfer between indications automatically.
  • Standard efficacy endpoint: ASAS40.

Integrated Disease Ecosystem Map: Alzheimer's

Brief: Built an outside-in map of the Alzheimer's disease ecosystem for a blood-based biomarker launch: care pathways and where the biomarker fits, patient personas from social listening and literature, and a patient-funnel model sizing the US biomarker opportunity with scenario analysis to support the client's market access strategy. [OUTCOME: e.g. adopted as the base case for the US launch plan / informed pricing and reimbursement submissions.]

Situation
  • Client preparing to launch a blood-based biomarker for Alzheimer's disease into the US market.
  • Anti-amyloid therapies had made biological confirmation of Alzheimer's a gate to treatment, and the existing routes, amyloid PET and CSF, were expensive, invasive and capacity-constrained.
  • A blood test could move confirmation earlier and into primary care, but the client needed an outside-in view: how patients actually travel through the system, where a blood test would slot in, who the patients are, and how large the realistic US opportunity was.
Complication
  • No single source describes the pathway: diagnosis is spread across primary care, memory clinics, neurology and geriatrics, with long and variable delays between first symptoms and a confirmed diagnosis.
  • Published epidemiology counts prevalence, not the funnel: who presents, who gets screened, who is referred and who reaches a biomarker test are all different, smaller numbers.
  • Patient voice is scattered across forums, social media and advocacy groups, with caregivers often speaking for patients.
  • The market was moving as we worked: the first blood-based tests were reaching FDA clearance, and guidelines on their use were still forming, so the sizing had to hold under several adoption scenarios rather than one.
Solution
  1. Mapped the current US care pathway end to end, from first symptoms and primary-care screening through specialist referral, amyloid confirmation and treatment eligibility, and marked the decision points where a blood-based biomarker could replace or precede PET and CSF.
  2. Built five patient personas from social listening, selected literature and expert interviews, covering stage of disease, how they entered the system, caregiver involvement and attitudes to testing.
  3. Modelled the patient funnel: US population at risk, prevalence of cognitive impairment, share presenting to care, screened, referred, biomarker-eligible, tested and amyloid-positive, with each conversion rate sourced and flagged by confidence.
  4. Ran scenario analysis on the levers the client could influence or needed to plan for: primary-care vs. specialist adoption, reimbursement timing, and uptake of anti-amyloid therapy.
  5. Delivered the ecosystem map and the funnel model as the evidence base for the client's market access strategy.
Impact
  • Client received a single, sourced view of the disease ecosystem: where patients are lost, where a blood test adds the most value, and who the realistic early adopters are.
  • Funnel model gave a defensible US opportunity range with the assumptions exposed, replacing top-down prevalence-based sizing.

Disease & market contextTap to open: Alzheimer's, blood biomarkers and the US market
Alzheimer's disease
  • Progressive neurodegenerative disease; the most common cause of dementia.
  • Defined biologically by amyloid plaques and tau tangles, which build up 10–20 years before symptoms.
  • ~7.4 million Americans aged 65+ live with Alzheimer's dementia (2026); many more are in the pre-symptomatic or mild cognitive impairment (MCI) stages.
Why biomarkers matter now
  • Anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab 2023, donanemab 2024) require confirmed amyloid pathology before treatment.
  • Confirmation has relied on amyloid PET (costly, limited scanner capacity) or CSF via lumbar puncture (invasive).
  • Blood tests measuring p-tau217 and amyloid ratios now approach PET-level accuracy; the first was FDA-cleared in 2025.
US market signals
  • Global blood-based Alzheimer's biomarker diagnostics market: ~$170M in 2025, projected to reach ~$840M by 2035 (~17% CAGR), with North America holding ~40% share.
  • Biomarker testing among Americans 55+ grew 37.5x between 2023 and 2025 following FDA clearance of blood tests.
  • 17% of newly diagnosed Alzheimer's patients had a biomarker test within six months of diagnosis in 2025, up from under 1% in 2023.
  • Diagnoses are shifting earlier: MCI diagnosis rates more than doubled between 2020 and 2025.

Sources: Towards Healthcare (2026); Trilliant Health (2026).

MoA Image Generator

Brief: Benchmarked 20+ AI image-generation tools for a top-10 pharma client paying ~$20K per mechanism-of-action (MoA) visual; shortlisted Gemini and GPT, built the prompt library and trained the team. Saving ~$80K a year, cutting briefing time ~25%, and now a product in testing on a colorectal cancer asset.

Situation
  • MoA visuals show how a drug acts on its target; they appear in MSL decks, congress posters, training and HCP content.
  • Client commissioned each from specialist medical illustrators at ~$20K per visual, with weeks of briefing and revision.
  • Generative image tools had become good enough to ask: can they produce scientifically acceptable MoA visuals, and if so which tool, and how?
Complication
  • Generic image quality was not the problem; scientific fidelity was: plausible-looking but wrong receptors and cascades, garbled text labels, inconsistent style across a series.
  • 20+ candidate tools and no standard way to compare them; output highly sensitive to prompt wording.
  • Client's medical, legal and regulatory review would catch errors late and expensively.
  • Medical writers who would use the tool had no prompt-engineering experience; a recommendation alone would change nothing.
Solution
  1. Built an assessment framework: scientific accuracy, text rendering, ease and reproducibility of prompting, stylistic consistency, iteration speed, cost.
  2. Ran 20+ tools through the framework on the same MoA briefs; shortlisted Gemini and GPT.
  3. Wrote the prompt library and testing guide: structured templates encoding the scientific content, labelling and style reviewers expect.
  4. Delivered prompt-engineering training to the medical writing team so they could brief and iterate visuals themselves.
  5. Handed framework and prompt library to the development team as the product specification.
Impact
  • Replacing the $20K-per-visual spend; ~$80K saved per year.
  • Briefing time per visual down ~25%.
  • Now a product: the MoA Image Generator takes an asset name, pulls MoA steps from PubMed, drafts a structured prompt with an LLM for review, and generates the visuals. In testing on a colorectal cancer asset.

Domain contextTap to open: what MoA visuals are and why AI struggles with them
What an MoA visual is
  • Illustrates how a drug works at the molecular or cellular level: target bound, pathway blocked or activated, downstream effect on disease.
  • Used across MSL decks, congress posters, HCP education and internal training.
  • Must survive medical, legal and regulatory (MLR) review before use.
Why they cost so much
  • Commissioned from specialist medical illustrators, briefed by a medical writer, revised over several rounds with scientific reviewers.
  • Cost is driven by accuracy and review burden, not drawing: a single image can run to $20K and several weeks.
Why generative AI struggles
  • Models are trained on general imagery, not validated biology: plausible but incorrect receptors, pathways and cell structures.
  • Historically poor text-label rendering; hard to keep a consistent style across a series.
  • Usable output depends on structured prompting plus a review step, so tool choice, a prompt library and training matter as much as the model.
